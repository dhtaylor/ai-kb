## IA Review — Recommendation on APAC Expansion: Thailand Entry Decision

**Reviewed at:** Full level — this is a formal capital-allocation recommendation to management (a $1.2M budget approval with a defined go/no-go gate), structured as a finished analytic product (BLUF / Background / Assessment / Alternatives / Recommendation).

**Analytic line (BLUF as written):** "We judge, with moderate confidence, that Thailand is the right first APAC market for a FY2026 entry. The risk-adjusted opportunity outweighs the alternatives reviewed; however, if partner-channel depth proves shallower than scoped, the bear case favors deferral to FY2027."

### Strengths
- **BLUF is genuinely first, and it's a complete one.** The opening sentence carries the judgment, a calibrated confidence level, *and* the condition that would flip it — "if partner-channel depth proves shallower than scoped, the bear case favors deferral to FY2027." That's ATS #6 and #2 done together, not just a well-worded headline (Standard #6, #2).
- **The rejected alternative is weighed on a real mechanism, not dismissed by assertion.** Philippines is engaged with a specific causal chain — "Philippine enterprise buying cycles run approximately 20% longer... which delays breakeven by roughly one quarter and reduces NPV at our 12% hurdle rate" — rather than a generic "Thailand is better" (Standard #4).
- **The load-bearing assumption is surfaced and confidence is pinned to it, not buried.** "Confidence is moderate: the Indonesia analogy is directionally sound but subject to Thai-specific channel dynamics we have not yet fully mapped" names exactly which input is doing the work and how solid it is — plus the recommendation converts to concrete, falsifiable thresholds ("pipeline coverage ratio ≥3× and partner NPS ≥40") rather than a vague "we'll monitor progress" (Standard #3, #5).

### Weaknesses (most to least threatening to the conclusion)

1. **Unexamined motivation behind the two most decision-critical primary sources** — Standard #1 (sourcing quality)
   > "Our own customer-discovery interviews (n=18 Thai enterprise buyers, Q1 2025, conducted by our regional BD team)" / "our regional partner survey (Q4 2024, n=12 resellers)"
   Why it matters: these two data points carry the entire comparative case — the BD-team-run interviews supply the Thailand willingness-to-pay figure that makes the market attractive, and the reseller survey supplies the buying-cycle gap that knocks out the strongest alternative. Both sources have a plausible stake in the outcome (a BD team benefits from expansion approval; resellers surveyed about entry timing may not be neutral toward which market gets the budget), and ICD 203's standard is explicit that sources be assessed for "possible bias/motivation," not just cited with an n and a date. Sample size and date are given; independence is not.
   Fix: Run a Quality-of-Information check on these two sources specifically — state whether the BD team or the resellers have a stake in the Thailand-vs-Philippines call, and if so, treat the willingness-to-pay and buying-cycle figures as directional bounds rather than point estimates, or add one independent corroborating source (e.g., a third-party Thai buyer survey) before the go/no-go review.

2. **Asymmetric hedging between the favored and rejected hypotheses** — Standard #4 / Analysis of Competing Hypotheses
   > "delays breakeven by roughly one quarter and reduces NPV at our 12% hurdle rate" (Philippines) vs. "directionally sound but subject to Thai-specific channel dynamics we have not yet fully mapped" (Thailand)
   Why it matters: the same document that carefully hedges its own favored candidate's weakest input states the case against the runner-up in flat, unhedged, precise-sounding terms — from a 12-respondent sample. That is the ACH failure mode Heuer warns about: evidence is scrutinized harder when it threatens the preferred conclusion than when it supports rejecting the alternative. If the Philippines breakeven-delay estimate is actually as soft as its n=12 base implies, the "second-best" gap may be smaller than stated.
   Fix: Give the Philippines estimate the same treatment as the Thailand one — state it as a range with a confidence level ("approximately one quarter, low confidence, based on a 12-reseller sample not yet cross-checked with buyer-side data") — and re-run the NPV comparison with that uncertainty carried through, rather than treating one hypothesis's numbers as provisional and the other's as settled.

3. **One comparative claim carries no source at all, in an otherwise carefully sourced memo** — Standard #1 (sourcing quality)
   > "its lower regulatory complexity relative to Vietnam"
   Why it matters: every other quantitative claim in the memo is attributed (IDC report, interview n and date, cohort n and vintage, partner survey n and date) — this one is asserted bare and folded directly into "make it the leading candidate." It's a smaller thread than #1 and #2 since Vietnam isn't the seriously-contested alternative (Philippines is), but an unsupported comparative claim shouldn't be doing ranking work in a capital decision.
   Fix: Either cite the specific regulatory dimension being compared (licensing, FX repatriation, data residency, foreign-ownership caps) or drop it as a stated basis for the ranking.

### If I were the analyst, the one change I'd make first
Before this goes to the go/no-go committee, add one line disclosing that the willingness-to-pay interviews and the reseller survey were run by parties with a stake in the answer, and re-state the Philippines breakeven-delay figure with the same confidence hedge given to Thailand's own CAC:LTV assumption. That single pass would tell management how much of "moderate confidence" is actually resting on independently-checked evidence versus internally-sourced numbers on both sides of the comparison.
