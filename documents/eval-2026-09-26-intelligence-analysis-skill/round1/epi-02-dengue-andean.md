## IA Review — Dengue Transmission Risk, Andean Region, Q3 2025 (WHO/PAHO Regional Surveillance Unit)

**Reviewed at:** Full level — a formal WHO/PAHO regional forecast, addressed to health ministries, that drives a resource-allocation decision (vector control supply pre-positioning); consequential and externally read.
**Analytic line (bottom line as written):** "Dengue transmission across Colombia, Peru, and Ecuador will likely remain above the 10-year seasonal median through August 2025 (assessed probability: ~60–70%), driven by residual DENV-3 serotype circulation and above-average precipitation in lowland endemic zones."

### Strengths
- Calibrated bottom line, positioned first, using a ladder-consistent term — "will likely remain above the 10-year seasonal median... (assessed probability: ~60–70%)" matches the ODNI ICD 203 ladder's "Likely/probable" band (55–80%) exactly, satisfying "Uses clear and logical argumentation" and "Properly expresses and explains uncertainties."
- Confidence is stated as a separate axis from likelihood and tied to a named driver of uncertainty — "Overall analytic confidence is moderate, reflecting El Niño–La Niña transition uncertainty" — exactly the combined form the library's writing-craft file prescribes, rather than the common failure of conflating the two.
- Explicitly reconciles this judgment with prior analytic coverage — "This updates and revises upward our March 2025 assessment (50–60%), reflecting a confirmed serotype shift and April precipitation anomalies" — satisfying "Explains change to or consistency of analytic judgments," which applies here because prior coverage exists to compare against.

### Weaknesses (most to least threatening to the conclusion)

1. **Revision magnitude looks anchored to the prior estimate** — Makes accurate judgments and assessments (ATS #8)
   > "This updates and revises upward our March 2025 assessment (50–60%), reflecting a confirmed serotype shift and April precipitation anomalies."
   Why it matters: a *confirmed* serotype shift (which typically signals a population with less immunity to the newly-dominant serotype) and confirmed precipitation anomalies are the kind of evidence that would usually warrant re-deriving the estimate from the base rate up, not nudging it one notch. The cognitive-biases library names this exact pattern — "an updated estimate that barely moves from the prior one despite newly cited risks/evidence" — as the tell-tale of **Anchoring**, to be named as such rather than treated as a reasonable update. Since the pre-positioning deadline in the So-What rests on this 60–70% figure, an anchored rather than freshly-derived number weakens the basis for the whole recommendation.
   Fix: Re-estimate the probability from scratch — start from the 10-year seasonal base rate, then weigh the confirmed serotype shift and precipitation anomalies against it as if the March figure did not exist — and only then compare the two numbers. If they land close together, say why explicitly; don't let proximity to the prior stand unexamined. This is the library's prescribed mitigation for Anchoring: "re-estimate from scratch; use ranges; be wary of inherited prior judgments as anchors."

2. **The "low-regret" claim rests on an unsourced, unflagged assumption** — Properly distinguishes underlying information from analysts' assumptions and judgments (ATS #3)
   > "Stocks pre-positioned under the baseline scenario remain available for 2026 at minimal carrying cost if the alternative materializes... the decision is low-regret under either scenario."
   Why it matters: every epidemiological claim in this product is sourced and reliability-rated (PAHO/MoH case reports, ECMWF forecasts), but the "minimal carrying cost" / cross-year availability claim that the entire recommendation's low-regret framing depends on is stated as flat fact, with no signal phrase ("we assess/judge") and no basis given. If vector-control supplies carry real storage cost, shelf-life limits, or opportunity cost, the low-regret framing — and with it the case for committing resources now rather than waiting on the ~30%-probability alternative — collapses.
   Fix: Run a Key Assumptions Check on the So-What: state the carrying-cost/shelf-life claim explicitly as an assumption, verify it against actual vector-control supply-chain data, and either cite the basis or caveat it as unverified rather than folding it into the recommendation as settled fact.
