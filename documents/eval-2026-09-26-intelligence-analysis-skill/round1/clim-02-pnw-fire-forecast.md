## IA Review — Pacific Northwest Wildfire Season Outlook

**Reviewed at:** Full level — a named seasonal forecast bulletin, externally read by fire-management/planning stakeholders, with operational consequences riding on the call; the complete rubric applies (icd-203-tradecraft-standards.md).

**Analytic line (bottom line as written):** "We assess with moderate confidence (60–75%) that the 2024 Pacific Northwest wildfire season will exceed the 10-year average burned area."

### Strengths
- **Source characterization, not just citation** — satisfies Standard #1, "Properly describes quality and credibility of sources, data, and methods." The product doesn't just name NOAA and University of Washington as sources, it grades them: "University of Washington ensemble runs (research-grade, single institution — treat with commensurate caution; no independent corroboration cited here)." That is exactly the source-descriptor move the standard calls for, applied to the weaker of two inputs rather than the stronger one.
- **Real analysis of alternatives, including a high-impact/low-probability check** — satisfies Standard #4, "Incorporates analysis of alternatives." The wet-pattern scenario ("a wet June–July pattern analogous to 2011 — would suppress fire activity regardless of snowpack deficit... roughly 25% probability") is a genuine competing outcome, not a token one, and its probability is roughly complementary to the main call (60–75% + 25% ≈ 100%), which is internally consistent. Key Judgment 3's dismissal of a 2020-Labor-Day-style catastrophic season is likewise a proper High-Impact/Low-Probability treatment (structured-analytic-techniques.md) — it doesn't just wave the scenario off, it names the specific missing preconditions ("specific ignition-timing and wind conditions not captured in seasonal outlooks") that would have to hold.
- **Bottom line in position, and information kept separate from judgment** — satisfies Standard #6 ("clear and logical argumentation," judged by position, not just clarity) and Standard #3. The judgment is the second line of the document under an explicit "BOTTOM LINE UP FRONT" header, not buried after throat-clearing context. And the evidence is kept visibly separate from the inference drawn from it: "Snowpack deficit correlates with regional burned area at r ≈ 0.61 over 1990–2023; substantial interannual noise remains and the relationship is not deterministic" reports the correlation and immediately flags its limits, rather than presenting it as settled proof.

### Weaknesses (most to least threatening to the conclusion)
1. **Confidence and likelihood are conflated in the bottom line** — Standard #2, "Properly expresses and explains uncertainties" (are likelihood and confidence stated separately, not conflated?).
   > "We assess with moderate confidence (60–75%) that the 2024 Pacific Northwest wildfire season will exceed the 10-year average burned area."
   Why it matters: confidence (how good the evidence base is) and likelihood (how probable the event is) are orthogonal axes — the library's own worked example states them separately: "We assess it is very likely (≈85%) that X; confidence is moderate, resting on..." Here the numeric range sits immediately next to the word "confidence," so a reader can't tell whether 60–75% is the probability of the fire season exceeding average or a number attached to how much evidence backs the call — and confidence is defined as categorical (High/Moderate/Low) only, never a percentage. This is the document's headline sentence, so the ambiguity lands on the single number a decision-maker is most likely to act on.
   Fix: split the two axes explicitly, in the library's combined form: "We judge it is likely (60–75%) that the 2024 PNW wildfire season will exceed the 10-year average burned area; confidence in this judgment is moderate, resting on operationally validated NOAA seasonal guidance and a single, as-yet-uncorroborated university ensemble." Nothing about the underlying assessment needs to change — only which word the range is attached to.

### Overall read
This is a disciplined short-form product. It gets the hard parts of tradecraft right for its length — it characterizes its weakest source rather than hiding it, runs a real alternative-scenario check including a high-impact/low-probability case, leads with the judgment, and keeps reported correlation visibly separate from the conclusion drawn from it. It has one fixable expression defect, and it's the one that matters most because it sits in the headline sentence: the confidence label and the probability range are welded together in a way the standard treats as two separate things. Fixing the single sentence in the BLUF resolves it; nothing about the underlying evidence or judgment needs to change.

### If I were the analyst, the one change I'd make first
Rewrite the BLUF's opening clause to state likelihood and confidence as two separate statements — "it is likely (60–75%) that X; confidence in this judgment is moderate, resting on ___" — so the one number a reader takes away is unambiguous.
