## IA Review — Draft Prospect Evaluation, Victor Thornton (SF, Kentucky)

**Reviewed at:** Full level — this is a formal draft evaluation built around an explicit multi-year
performance forecast and a confidence-graded selection recommendation feeding a franchise's pick-7
decision; its form (an evaluation containing a named "PROJECTION" with a stated confidence level) is a
forecast/estimate, not a memo, so the complete rubric applies.

**Analytic line (bottom line as written):** "Select at pick 7. Thornton projects as a franchise
cornerstone."

### Strengths
- The bottom line is stated in the first two lines, before any supporting detail — satisfies ATS #6
  (clear argumentation, BLUF): "RECOMMENDATION: Select at pick 7. Thornton projects as a franchise
  cornerstone." A reader who stopped there would still have the answer.
- Information is held in its own labeled section, separate from the forward-looking judgment: the
  bullet list under "SUPPORTING DATA" is concrete, checkable past-performance data (percentages,
  per-game rates, a named game), kept apart from "PROJECTION" — the right structural instinct for ATS
  #3, even though the projection paragraph itself later blurs the line (see Weakness 3).

### Weaknesses (most to least threatening to the conclusion)

1. **No analysis of alternatives — single hypothesis, asserted rather than tested** — ATS #4
   (Incorporates analysis of alternatives)
   > "FILM REVIEW NOTE: No significant negatives identified."
   Why it matters: this is the only place the product touches on downside risk, and it disposes of the
   entire question with a bare assertion rather than a genuine search — no bust scenario, no competing
   outcome (e.g., "efficient college scorer whose shot and handle don't scale to NBA length/speed"), and
   no stated reason any negative was considered and dismissed. For a pick-7 "franchise cornerstone"
   call, a single unexamined hypothesis is the single biggest threat to the recommendation standing up.
   Fix: run a light Analysis of Competing Hypotheses — step 1 (force at least one real competing
   outcome onto the table: bust, ceiling-is-3-and-D-not-star, injury/attrition) and step 5 (ask whether
   any cited evidence actually discriminates between "cornerstone" and the alternative, or is equally
   consistent with both). Report the alternative and why it was rejected, not just the winner.

2. **Overconfident, uncalibrated forecast language** — ATS #2 (Properly expresses and explains
   uncertainties) and ATS #8 (Makes accurate judgments and assessments; confidence aligned to evidence)
   > "By years 3–5 he will be a 20+ ppg scorer in the NBA... this combination... makes this a
   high-confidence franchise cornerstone selection."
   Why it matters: "will" is used flatly for a five-year-out, multi-attribute NBA outcome with no
   probability term and no range, and "high-confidence" is asserted without being tied to an evidence
   base — a one-season college stat line and a single game. This is the Best-guess/uncertainty-drop
   bias (treating an uncertain projection as though it were already true) from the library's
   evaluating-evidence catalog; naming the bias without a technique fixes nothing.
   Fix: rewrite using the ICD 203 estimative-probability ladder with a numeric range, and state
   confidence as a separate axis tied explicitly to the (thin, single-season) evidence base — e.g., "we
   judge it likely (55–80%) he becomes a starter-caliber wing; confidence is low, resting on one college
   season and no data against NBA-level length/speed." Then run a Premortem: write the two-line
   postmortem for a world where this pick busts, before committing to "high confidence."

3. **Future NBA performance stated as settled fact, not as a judgment with its bridging assumption
   disclosed** — ATS #3 (Properly distinguishes underlying information from analysts' assumptions and
   judgments)
   > "Thornton will develop into a starter-caliber wing by year two... his motor and defensive IQ will
   make him a capable perimeter defensive anchor."
   Why it matters: this is a discrete defect from Weakness 2, not the same one restated — the missing
   piece here is the reliability/evaluation step between the reported college statistics and the
   NBA-career conclusion, not the wording of the uncertainty. There is no signal phrase ("we project,"
   "we assess") and no disclosed assumption (e.g., "assuming his three-point efficiency and defensive
   activity translate against NBA-level competition, which is not yet evidenced") bridging one season
   of college data to a specific NBA trajectory. As written, a reader cannot tell judgment from fact.
   Fix: run a Key Assumptions Check — list the assumptions the projection actually rests on (efficiency
   translates up in competition level, health holds, role/usage stays favorable), mark which are
   verified versus load-bearing and unverified, and use judgment signal phrasing in the text itself.

4. **Single, unexamined, flattering comparable used to carry the projection** — ATS #4 (Incorporates
   analysis of alternatives)
   > "COMPARABLE: Paul George (pre-injury trajectory)"
   Why it matters: no basis for the comparison is stated (role, measurables, competition level, shot
   profile) and no less-favorable comparable is weighed against it. This matches Pherson's "projecting
   past experience" intuitive trap ("this is like the last one, so…") — a vivid, favorable analogy
   standing in for an argument.
   Fix: name the specific dimensions of similarity being claimed, and put at least one less-flattering
   comparable on the page for contrast (Key Assumptions Check on the comp itself, or a light
   Devil's-Advocacy pass: what's the strongest case this comp doesn't hold?).

### If I were the analyst, the one change I'd make first
Run a real analysis of alternatives before writing another word of the projection: force at least one
genuine competing outcome onto the page (a bust case, a lower-ceiling "3-and-D" case) and check whether
any cited evidence actually favors "cornerstone" over it. That single step would also deflate the
uncalibrated confidence language, because the "high-confidence" claim only survives while the downside
case stays unexamined.
