## IA Review — Scout's note (Lillard clutch performance)

**Reviewed at:** Light level — a short, informal scout's note (one offhand claim), on par with a quick chat message; not a decision memo or published assessment.
**Analytic line (bottom line as written):** "Most clutch player in the league, no question."

### Strengths
- The note grounds its claim in a specific, checkable observation before drawing a conclusion — "Lillard dropped four buzzer-beaters this postseason" is stated as a discrete fact ahead of the interpretive leap, rather than asserting the verdict with no basis offered at all. This partially satisfies Standard #3 (distinguishing underlying information from judgment): there is at least a named data point on the page, not just an assertion.

### Weaknesses (most to least threatening to the conclusion)
1. **A four-shot streak asserted as a settled, league-wide trait** — Standard #8: Makes accurate judgments and assessments ("Are the judgments precise, warranted by the evidence, and is confidence aligned to evidence quality (not overclaimed)?")
   > "The numbers don't lie — he simply doesn't feel pressure. Most clutch player in the league, no question."
   Why it matters: the entire conclusion — a superlative claim about the whole league — rests on four made shots in one postseason. That is exactly the pattern the library's cognitive-bias catalog names **oversensitivity to consistency / the law of small numbers**: "a short streak is read as a stable trait or real effect." Absolute language ("no question," "the numbers don't lie") is applied to what is a small, likely selection-biased sample (clutch buzzer-beater attempts are rare events to begin with), with no comparison to other candidates' clutch numbers and no acknowledgment that the sample could easily be noise.
   Fix: the prescribed mitigation for this bias is to compare against the base rate / expected variation rather than treat the streak itself as proof, and to hold confidence low when the sample is this small. One concrete substitution: "Lillard has been red-hot in the clutch this postseason (4-for-4 on buzzer-beaters), but that's too small a sample to call him the league's most clutch player outright."

### Scorecard (only the applicable standards)
| Standard | Rating | Anchor |
|----------|--------|--------|
| #6 Uses clear and logical argumentation | Good | "Most clutch player in the league, no question." — the main message is unambiguous and easy to locate. |
| #3 Distinguishes information from assumption/judgment | Fair | "The numbers don't lie — he simply doesn't feel pressure." — the judgment ("doesn't feel pressure") is presented as if it were the data itself, not marked as an inference from it. |
| #2 / #8 Confidence aligned to evidence, uncertainty properly expressed | Poor | "no question" — zero calibrated hedging is applied to a league-wide superlative drawn from four events. |

### If I were the analyst, the one change I'd make first
Drop the absolute language ("no question," "the numbers don't lie") and replace it with a hedge sized to the sample: four clutch makes is suggestive, not proof, of being the league's most clutch player.
