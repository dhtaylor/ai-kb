## IA Review — Research Brief: mRNA Flu Vaccine Efficacy — Phase II Preliminary Data

**Reviewed at:** Medium level — a short, single-page research brief built around one bottom line and
supporting reasoning (the form of a memo), addressed to a reader weighing a policy-relevant question;
its form, not its subject's stakes, sets the level.
**Analytic line (bottom line as written):** "Early Phase II data suggest mRNA-based flu vaccines may
produce a stronger antibody response than traditional egg-derived formulations, but the evidence is
immunogenicity-only and is insufficient to support a policy change recommendation."

### Strengths
- **Bottom line up front, correctly positioned.** The judgment opens the product in the first sentence,
  not after background or chronology — satisfies Standard #6 ("Uses clear and logical argumentation …
  clear main message (BLUF) up front").
- **The primary source is genuinely characterized, not just cited.** "The trial sponsor has a commercial
  interest in the outcome; independent replication has not been published" — this hits bias/motivation
  and corroboration, the exact dimensions Standard #1 asks for, for the one source that drives the
  judgment.
- **Information and judgment are kept visibly separate.** The reported trial result ("generated a
  geometric mean titer 2.1× higher … at day 28") is never blurred into the conclusion; the conclusion
  itself is explicitly hedged as insufficient — satisfies Standard #3.

### Weaknesses (most to least threatening to the conclusion)

1. **Ambiguous probability language in the bottom line itself** — Standard #2, "Properly expresses and
   explains uncertainties"
   > "Early Phase II data suggest mRNA-based flu vaccines **may** produce a stronger antibody response"
   Why it matters: "may" is exactly the undefined, no-direction hedge the library's estimative-probability
   ladder flags as the single most common uncertainty failure ("might/may flags uncertainty with no
   direction"). The brief has already assembled everything needed for a calibrated statement — a single
   trial, a sponsor conflict, no independent replication — but never converts those into a likelihood
   term plus a separately stated confidence level. The reader can't tell whether the author means "worth
   watching" or "probably real but unproven," and that ambiguity sits in the one sentence carrying the
   whole brief's claim.
   Fix: Replace the hedge with the library's combined form — likelihood term (with range) stated
   separately from confidence, tied to the evidence base. E.g.: "We assess it is roughly even chance to
   likely (45–65%) that mRNA platforms produce meaningfully stronger antibody responses than inactivated
   vaccines; confidence is low, resting on one sponsor-funded trial with no independent replication."

2. **One-sided source characterization** — Standard #1, "Properly describes quality and credibility of
   sources, data, and methods"
   > "The incumbent technology retains several advantages: a longer real-world safety record, single-dose
   annual administration compatible with existing campaign infrastructure, and established cold-chain
   logistics."
   Why it matters: the mRNA trial gets full characterization — sponsor, sample size, journal, date,
   conflict of interest, replication status. The incumbent's advantages get none of that: no citation, no
   basis given for "longer... safety record" or "established... logistics." This is the master mapping's
   named pattern — "overconfident or one-sided sourcing" — applied asymmetrically to favor the status quo
   the brief ends up recommending sticking with, which is precisely the kind of one-sidedness Standard #1
   and the overarching "Objective" standard exist to catch.
   Fix: Run a Quality-of-Information Check on the incumbent-advantages paragraph the same way it was run
   on the trial — name what the safety-record and cold-chain claims rest on (e.g., which surveillance
   data, which campaigns), or mark them as background assertion rather than evidence-grade claims.

### Scorecard (only the applicable standards)
| Standard | Rating | Anchor |
|----------|--------|--------|
| #1 Sources, data, and methods properly characterized | Fair | Trial: "commercial interest ... independent replication has not been published" (Good) vs. incumbent-advantage claims carrying no basis at all (the gap) |
| #2 Uncertainty properly expressed | Fair | "may produce a stronger antibody response" — undefined direction-less hedge, no calibrated term or confidence statement |
| #3 Information vs. assumption vs. judgment | Good | "generated a geometric mean titer 2.1× higher" (reported) kept distinct from "insufficient to support a policy change recommendation" (judgment) |
| #4 Analysis of alternatives | Good | The incumbent's dosing, logistics, and safety-record advantages are weighed as a genuine competing consideration, not waved off |
| #5 Customer relevance / implications | Good | Directly answers the implied decision question — "a recommendation to shift national formulation policy would be premature" |
| #6 Clear and logical argumentation | Good | Bottom line opens the product; the reasoning that follows (endpoint mismatch, no replication, incumbent trade-offs) actually supports it |
| #8 Accurate, warranted judgments | Good | Conclusion is no more confident than the immunogenicity-only, unreplicated, sponsor-funded evidence warrants |

### If I were the analyst, the one change I'd make first
Rewrite the bottom line's "may produce a stronger antibody response" into the library's calibrated form —
a likelihood term with a range, plus a confidence level stated separately and tied explicitly to the
single-trial, sponsor-funded, unreplicated evidence base already described later in the brief. That one
sentence carries the whole product, and it's the only place the otherwise-careful hedging doesn't show.
