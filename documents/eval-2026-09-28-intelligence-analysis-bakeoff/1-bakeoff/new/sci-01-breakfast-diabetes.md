## IA Review — sci-01-breakfast-diabetes

**Reviewed at:** Full level — its form (a published science-news report explicitly calling for a national dietary-guideline change) is not named in the Light or Medium lists, so it falls to the "consequential or externally read" catch-all: it is stated as already published ("published this week") and is driving a real policy ask ("Public-health advocates are already calling for national dietary guidelines to be updated").

**Analytic line (bottom line as written):** "Researchers said the findings demonstrate that skipping breakfast disrupts morning metabolic rhythms, driving insulin resistance over time" (reinforced by the quote: "The data make clear that eating breakfast is protective against diabetes.")

### Strengths
- Leads with the concrete finding in the opening sentence rather than background or throat-clearing — satisfies the clear-bottom-line-up-front check (Standard #6: "a clear main message (BLUF) up front"). Quote: "A large observational study ... found that regular breakfast eaters were 38% less likely to develop type 2 diabetes."
- Characterizes the underlying study's basic evidentiary parameters rather than asserting a bare number — names the venue, cohort size, and duration (partially meets Standard #1: "properly describes quality and credibility of sources, data, and methods"). Quote: "published this week in Nature Communications followed 12,400 adults for five years."

### Weaknesses (most to least threatening to the conclusion)

1. **Correlational finding recast as causal fact** — Standard #3 ("properly distinguishes underlying information from analysts' assumptions and judgments") and Standard #8 ("makes accurate judgments... confidence aligned to evidence quality, not overclaimed")
   > "Researchers said the findings demonstrate that skipping breakfast disrupts morning metabolic rhythms, driving insulin resistance over time." / "The data make clear that eating breakfast is protective against diabetes."
   Why it matters: the study is explicitly described as observational, which cannot by itself establish a causal mechanism. The information (a 38% relative-risk difference) is collapsed directly into a causal judgment with no signal phrase ("we assess," "likely") and no hedge — the exact failure the library's worked example names: a reported number becoming a confirmed conclusion.
   Fix: run the Quality-of-Information Check (audit the study's method against the strength of the claim being hung on it — an observational design supports an association claim, not a mechanism claim) and rewrite using the ICD 203 estimative-probability ladder, e.g., "the study found breakfast eating is associated with lower diabetes incidence; researchers believe, but the design cannot confirm, that this reflects a metabolic mechanism."

2. **No alternative explanation considered for the association** — Standard #4 ("incorporates analysis of alternatives")
   > absent: no confounding factor, reverse-causation possibility, or competing explanation appears anywhere in the product.
   Why it matters: breakfast-skippers and breakfast-eaters plausibly differ on other health-relevant traits (activity level, shift work, smoking, baseline health, socioeconomic status) that could independently explain the diabetes-risk gap; without ruling this out, the causal story in finding 1 has no support beyond the correlation itself. This also matches Pherson's "satisficing" intuitive trap — stopping at the first plausible mechanism rather than testing it against a rival one.
   Fix: run a light Analysis of Competing Hypotheses pass — state the confounding-lifestyle hypothesis alongside the metabolic-disruption hypothesis and ask whether any cited evidence actually discriminates between them (it does not, since only the correlation is reported).

3. **Single uncorroborated study used to justify a national policy recommendation** — overarching standard "based on all available sources" and Standard #1 ("properly describes quality and credibility of sources... corroboration")
   > "Public-health advocates are already calling for national dietary guidelines to be updated to formally recommend daily breakfast."
   Why it matters: the piece names exactly one study as the entire evidentiary basis for a national guideline change, with no mention of prior literature, replication, or conflicting findings — the library's sourcing standard treats an uncorroborated single source as a distinct weakness even when the source itself is well-described.
   Fix: apply the Quality-of-Information Check's corroboration test before the recommendation stands — name what other studies say (or that none are cited), and scale the policy language to "one study suggests" rather than treating it as settled.

### If I were the analyst, the one change I'd make first
Rewrite the causal sentence as a properly hedged association and name the confounding alternative explicitly — that single fix removes the unsupported mechanism claim (finding 1) and, by surfacing what the study can't rule out, also defuses the unearned jump to a national policy recommendation (finding 3).
