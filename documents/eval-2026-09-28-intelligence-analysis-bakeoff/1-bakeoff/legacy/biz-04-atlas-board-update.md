## IA Review — Project Atlas Board Update (Q2 2025)

**Reviewed at:** Full level — a board of directors is being asked to approve continued spend on the strength of a 12-month, $8M ARR forecast and a "no course corrections needed" recommendation; this is a consequential, externally-read governance document, not an internal working memo.

**Analytic line (BLUF as written):** "Project Atlas... is on track for a Q4 2025 General Availability launch... we project Atlas will generate $8M in ARR within 12 months of GA."

### Strengths
- **BLUF is genuinely up front.** The launch status and the headline $8M figure both appear in the first two sentences of the Executive Summary, before any supporting detail — satisfies ATS #6's position test cleanly.
- **Part of the ARR math shows its work.** "Three committed beta converters (at our $120K average ACV = $360K)" and "seven of those 14 — a 50% close rate — yields $840K" are traceable, arithmetic-checkable, and tied to named accounts — a real instance of separating a computed judgment from a bare assertion (ATS #3), for that slice of the number.
- **Budget reporting is concrete and falsifiable.** "$2.1M spent against a $3.0M total authorization" is a verifiable fact, not a vibe — good practice to carry forward into the rest of the memo.

### Weaknesses (most to least threatening to the conclusion)

1. **The majority of the headline number is an unsupported plug, not a projection** — Standard #1 (source/method quality) and #8 (accurate judgments)
   > "Converting seven of those 14 — a 50% close rate — yields $840K in year-one ARR. The remaining $6.8M is expected from new logo acquisition through our standard outbound motion."
   Why it matters: $6.8M of the $8M figure — 85% of the total — has no deal count, no historical outbound-motion conversion rate, no sales-cycle length, and no comparison to what "standard outbound motion" has actually produced for a brand-new product category. The memo applies real rigor (named accounts, an explicit close rate) to the 15% of the number that's easiest to defend, and none to the 85% that carries the recommendation. This is the load-bearing claim in the whole document, and it's the least examined one — remove it and the $8M figure collapses to $1.2M.
   Fix: Run a Key Assumptions Check on "$6.8M from new-logo acquisition": state the assumed deal count, ACV, and close rate that produce it, and name the historical outbound conversion rate (if any exists — Atlas has no track record yet, which is itself worth stating) it's extrapolated from.

2. **Every forecast in the memo is a bare point estimate — no probability or confidence stated anywhere** — Standard #2 (expresses uncertainty)
   > "we project Atlas will generate $8M in ARR within 12 months of GA" ... "Development is 82% complete... putting GA in early October"
   Why it matters: Both the revenue and the schedule are presented as near-facts rather than estimates with a stated likelihood and a separately stated confidence level. A board approving spend on this number has no way to tell whether $8M is a floor, a midpoint, or a stretch case — that materially changes whether "no course corrections" is the right call.
   Fix: State each forecast as a range with calibrated language and a confidence level tied to its evidence base, e.g. "we assess $6–9M ARR as likely (evidence for the $1.2M named-pipeline component is solid; the new-logo component is low-confidence, resting on no comparable track record)."

3. **Enthusiastic quotes are from motivated internal sources and are not corroborated** — Standard #1 (source characterization)
   > "Per our Head of Product: 'Atlas solves a problem enterprise customers have been waiting years for.'" ... "Our Customer Success team characterizes the response as 'best-in-class for an enterprise beta.'"
   Why it matters: Both sources are internal, invested in the product's success, and being cited to support the "resonating strongly" claim with no external benchmark for "best-in-class" and no discussion of whether the three verbal GA commitments (not signed contracts) are typical or fragile at this stage. Consistency between two internal stakeholders who share the same incentives is not corroboration.
   Fix: Note the sources' interest explicitly and add one independent data point (e.g., a customer-side quote, a signed LOI, or a survey conducted by someone outside the product/CS org) — or flag the $360K near-term figure as resting on unconverted verbal commitments until contracts are signed.

4. **No alternative timeline scenario despite being at the highest-risk stage of an enterprise software project** — Standard #4 (analysis of alternatives)
   > "Engineering estimates four more weeks of remaining work, putting GA in early October — six weeks ahead of our Q4 milestone."
   Why it matters: Linear extrapolation from "82% complete" is the classic planning-fallacy trap — final-stretch enterprise software work (integration testing, security review, customer-specific configuration) routinely runs longer than the preceding 82% would suggest, and the memo gives the board no sense of what could push GA past October or what the recommendation would look like under that case.
   Fix: Name the one or two most plausible sources of schedule slip (e.g., enterprise security review, beta-customer-specific integration work) and state whether they'd still leave GA inside Q4 even if they occur.

### If I were the analyst, the one change I'd make first
Before this goes to the board, break down the $6.8M new-logo component with the same rigor applied to the $1.2M named-pipeline component (assumed deal count, ACV, close rate, and the outbound motion's actual historical conversion rate) — and re-state the $8M headline as a range with a confidence level once that's done. Everything else in the memo is proportionate to fix only after that.
