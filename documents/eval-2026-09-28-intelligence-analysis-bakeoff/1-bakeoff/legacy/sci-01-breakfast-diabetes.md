## IA Review — sci-01-breakfast-diabetes (news item on breakfast/diabetes study)

**Reviewed at:** Medium level — a short news report (single paragraph, no formal structure) that nonetheless feeds a public-health policy recommendation; treated as a memo-tier product rather than a formal assessment given its length and format, but weighted toward the top of the Medium cap given the stakes (a call to change national dietary guidelines).

**Analytic line (BLUF as written):** "The data make clear that eating breakfast is protective against diabetes" (quoted from the lead author; the article's own framing echoes this in "the findings demonstrate that skipping breakfast disrupts morning metabolic rhythms, driving insulin resistance over time"). This appears early (by sentence 3 of 4) — not buried.

### Strengths
- Quantifies the finding and the study design rather than staying vague — "12,400 adults," "five years," "38% less likely" — giving a reader concrete numbers to evaluate, a basic form of Standard #1 transparency that many science write-ups skip.
- The main claim is stated early and unambiguously, not buried behind chronology or throat-clearing — satisfies the *position* half of Standard #6, whatever else is wrong with the claim itself.

### Weaknesses (most to least threatening to the conclusion)

1. **Correlation reported as causation** — Standard #3 (info vs. judgment) / #8 (accurate judgments)
   > "Researchers said the findings demonstrate that skipping breakfast disrupts morning metabolic rhythms, driving insulin resistance over time." / "'The data make clear that eating breakfast is protective against diabetes,' the lead author told reporters."
   Why it matters: this is the article's entire load-bearing claim, and it is a classic correlation-causation conflation (cum hoc ergo propter hoc). A five-year observational cohort can establish an association between breakfast habits and diabetes incidence; it cannot by itself "demonstrate" a metabolic mechanism or "make clear" that breakfast is protective. The causal language is not warranted by the study design described.
   Fix: Separate the reported statistic from the interpretation: "researchers found breakfast-skippers were 38% more likely to develop diabetes; they hypothesize this reflects a metabolic effect of meal timing, though the observational design cannot rule out other explanations." Run a Quality-of-Information check on whether any mechanistic (not just statistical) evidence backs the "disrupts morning metabolic rhythms" claim, or whether that's the lead author's unsupported gloss on a correlation.

2. **No analysis of alternatives — reverse causation and confounding never raised** — Standard #4
   > absent: nothing in the product addresses reverse causation or confounding anywhere.
   Why it matters: at least one competing explanation is at least as plausible as the article's and is never weighed: undiagnosed early insulin resistance can itself alter appetite and eating patterns, so people already on a path to diabetes may be more likely to skip breakfast (reverse causation) — or breakfast-skippers may differ systematically in ways that independently raise diabetes risk (shift work, lower SES, overall diet quality, BMI). Either would produce the same 38% figure with breakfast playing no causal role at all.
   Fix: Lay the two hypotheses side by side (light ACH): does the study report anything — e.g., adjustment for baseline glycemic markers, BMI, or socioeconomic status — that actually discriminates between "breakfast protects" and "the same underlying risk factors drive both diabetes and breakfast-skipping"? If not, say so explicitly rather than letting the causal framing stand unchallenged.

3. **Single study treated as sufficient basis for a policy call** — Standard #1 (quality/corroboration of sources)
   > "Public-health advocates are already calling for national dietary guidelines to be updated to formally recommend daily breakfast."
   Why it matters: one observational study, however large, is a thin evidentiary base for a guideline change. The article gives no indication of whether this replicates prior research or is an outlier, and reports the advocacy quote without any caveat about the strength of the underlying evidence — letting the policy ask ride on the causal misreading in Weakness 1.
   Fix: State the evidentiary weight plainly: note whether this is consistent with prior breakfast/diabetes research or a new finding awaiting replication, and flag that observational data of this kind would typically need corroborating (ideally interventional) evidence before supporting a guideline change.
